Example 2.3.12. Combinatorial proof — team and leader.

Use a combinatorial proof to prove that
\begin{equation*} \displaystyle 7\binom{10}{7} = 10 \binom{9}{6} \end{equation*}
🔗
Solution.
  Proof. We use a combinatorial proof. Consider the following situation: there is a group of ten employees. How many ways are there to choose seven employees to be on a project team where one member of the team is the project leader?
🔗
First, we can choose the seven-person project team from the group of ten employees. There are \(\binom{10}{7}\) ways to do this step. Then we need to choose one of those seven people on the project team to be the project leader. There are seven ways to do this step. (You could think of it as \(\binom{7}{1}\) ways, but \(\binom{7}{1}= 7\) and that is simpler.) Since steps multiply Theorem 2.2.2, the answer is \(7\binom{10}{7}\text{.}\)
🔗
On the other hand, we can make the choices in reverse order. We can start by choosing the project leader, one of the ten people. There are ten ways to do this step. Then we need to choose the rest of the project team. Since we already chose the project leader, we only need six more people out of the nine remaining people. There are \(\binom{9}{6}\) ways to do this step. Since steps multiply Theorem 2.2.2, the answer is also \(10 \binom{9}{6}\text{.}\)
🔗
Since we counted the same quantity in two different ways, it follows that \(7\binom{10}{7} = 10\binom{9}{6}\text{.}\)

    Section 9.5 Proofs of Explicit Formulas for Sums and Products

    We have observed patterns and conjectured explicit formulas for sums and products in Section 9.3, including sums involving Fibonacci numbers, and in Section 9.4, including sums involving binomial coefficients. In a few special cases, we saw how to use the Binomial Theorem to prove summation formulas, but how do we prove the Binomial Theorem itself as well as other formulas? In this section, we look at two proof techniques, including new special cases of mathematical induction and an extension of combinatorial proofs to summations. We close by looking at special sums, such as the handshake formula from Theorem 1.1.3 and finite geometric sums.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.5.A Proof Format: Special Case of Mathematical Induction for Proving Sum and Product Formulas

    We can use mathematical induction to prove explicit formulas for summations and products. We begin with the proof format for a summation.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Given the explicitly-defined sequence \(a_k\) for \(k \ge s\text{,}\) prove that \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^na_k=\) (explicit rule) for \(n \ge s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    Write \(f(n) =\)(explicit rule). Induct on \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First, when \(n=s\) we know that \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^sa_k=a_s= \cdots\) and also \(f(s)=\) (evaluate and simplify to get the same answer).
    🔗
    Next, assume \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^{n-1}a_k=f(n-1)=\) (evaluate and simplify), for some integer \(n > s\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=s}^na_k = \left(\sum_{k=s}^{n-1}a_k\right) + a_n = f(n-1) + a_n = \cdots \text{ (use algebra) } \cdots = f(n) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The key first step in the last line of the proof uses the recursive definitions of partial sums, Remark 9.3.5.
    🔗
    Similarly, here is the proof format for a product.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Given the explicitly-defined sequence \(a_k\) for \(k \ge s\text{,}\) prove that \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=s}^na_k=\) (explicit rule) for \(n \ge s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    Write \(f(n) =\)(explicit rule). Induct on \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First, when \(n=s\) we know that \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=s}^sa_k=a_s= \cdots\) and also \(f(s)=\) (evaluate and simplify to get the same answer).
    🔗
    Next, assume \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=s}^{n-1}a_k=f(n-1)=\) (evaluate and simplify), for some integer \(n > s\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \prod_{k=s}^na_k = \left(\prod_{k=s}^{n-1}a_k\right) \cdot a_n = f(n-1) \cdot a_n = \cdots \text{ (use algebra) } \cdots = f(n) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The key first step in the last line of the proof uses the recursive definitions of partial products, Remark 9.3.17.
    🔗
    As usual, when we introduce a new proof format, we give you examples to copy and edit.

    Subsection 9.5.B Proof Format: Combinatorial Proof of Sums

    We can use mathematical induction to prove the explicit formula of a sum of the form \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^na_k=f(n)\text{,}\) but the recursive step of these proofs is based on the fact that the terms \(a_k\) do not depend on the upper limit \(n\text{.}\) How can we prove an explicit formula for a summation where the terms depend on \(n\text{?}\) For example, in Section 9.4 we saw sums with binomial coefficients where the terms involved \(n\text{.}\) An option is to extend the proof format for a combinatorial proof, Theorem 2.3.11, to apply to sums. This extended proof format is especially useful for proofs involving binomial coefficients, since, by definition, they count objects.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Given the explicitly-defined sequence \(a_k\) for \(k \ge s\) where the terms \(a_k\) may depend on \(n\text{,}\) prove that \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^na_k=f(n)\) for \(n \ge s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    We use a combinatorial proof. Consider the following situation ….
    🔗
    How many ways are there to …?
    🔗
    First, condition on \(k=\) (say what \(k\) counts). In this case, there are (show how to get \(a_k\)) ways to do …. Since cases add Theorem 2.2.6, there are a total of \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^na_k\) ways.
    🔗
    On the other hand, (explain how to count using a different method to get \(f(n)\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Since we counted the same quantity in two different ways, it follows that \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=s}^na_k=f(n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The phrase “condition on \(k=\) (say what \(k\) counts)” indicates that we are considering a list of cases Case \(s\text{,}\) Case \(s+1\text{,}\) …, Case \(n\) where in Case \(k\text{,}\) there are \(k\) of some object. Let’s see this new proof format in action.

    Subsection 9.5.D Algebra: Fraction Arithmetic

    Some of our summation formulas involve fractions. Let’s review fraction arithmetic.
    🔗

    Definition 9.5.7. Adding Fractions.

     
    1. For integers \(n\text{,}\) \(m\text{,}\) and \(d\) with \(d \neq 0\text{,}\) the sum of the fractions is given by
      \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \frac{n}{d} + \frac{m}{d} = \frac{n+m}{d}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      For example,
      \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{7} + \frac{2}{7} = \frac{3+2}{7} = \frac{5}{7} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To add two fractions using (a), we need the two fractions to have the same denominator (common denominator). If two fractions have different denominators, we can replace one or both fractions with equivalent fractions that have a common denominator using Theorem 4.1.11.

      Solution.
      We can use \(k(k-1)\) as the common denominator.
      \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \frac{1}{k-1} - \frac{1}{k} = \frac{k(1)}{k(k-1)} - \frac{(k-1)(1)}{(k-1)(k)} = \frac{k}{k(k-1)} - \frac{k-1}{(k)(k-1)} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} = \frac{k-(k-1)}{(k)(k-1)} = \frac{k-k+1}{k(k-1)} = \frac{1}{k(k-1)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      By the way, it is sometimes useful to reverse this equation to write
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{k(k-1)} = \frac{1}{k-1} - \frac{1}{k} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Write as one fraction and simplify \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} + \frac{1}{2^k}\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      Recall that \(2\cdot 2^{k-1} =2^k\) so we can use \(2^k\) as the common denominator.
      \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} + \frac{1}{2^k} = \frac{2(1)}{2(2^{k-1})} + \frac{1}{2^k} = \frac{2}{2^k} + \frac{1}{2^k} = \frac{2+1}{2^k} = \frac{3}{2^k} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Write as one fraction and simplify \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{(n-1)!} + \frac{1}{n!}\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      Recall that \(n!=n(n-1)!\) so we can use \(n!\) as the common denominator to get
      \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \frac{1}{(n-1)!} + \frac{1}{n!} = \frac{n(1)}{n(n-1)!)} + \frac{1}{n!} = \frac{n}{n!} + \frac{1}{n!} = \frac{n+1}{n!} \end{equation*}

      🔗
      🔗

    Definition 9.5.9. Multiplying Fractions.

     
    1. For integers \(n, m, c, d\) with \(c,d \neq 0\text{,}\) the product of the fractions is given by
      \begin{equation*} \frac{m}{c} \cdot \frac{n}{d} = \frac{mn}{cd}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      For example,
      \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{7}{5} = \frac{(2)(7)}{(3)(5)} = \frac{14}{15}\text{.} \end{equation*}

    Example 9.5.10. Multiplying Fractions.

    In this example, we only use computational technology to check our answers.
    1. Write as one fraction and simplify: \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{5}{6}\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      By Definition 9.5.9,
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{5}{6} = \frac{(1)(5)}{(2)(6)} = \frac{5}{12}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗

      🔗

    2. Write as one fraction and simplify \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      We have
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{(1)(2)}{(2)(3)}= \frac{\cancel{2}(1)}{\cancel{2}(3)}=\frac{1}{3} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write as one fraction and simplify \(\displaystyle 5 \cdot \frac{3}{10}\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      To use Definition 9.5.9, we need to write 5 as the fraction \(\frac{5}{1}\text{.}\) Next,
      \begin{equation*} 5 \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{5}{1} \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{(5)(3)}{(1)(10)} = \frac{\cancel{5}(3)}{\cancel{5}(2)} = \frac{3}{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Simplify \(\displaystyle 4 \left(\frac{4^m-1}{3}\right)+1\)
      Solution.
      First, we can write 4 as \(\frac{4}{1}\) and multiply the fractions to get
      \begin{equation*} 4 \left(\frac{4^m-1}{3}\right)+1 = \frac{4}{1} \left(\frac{4^m-1}{3}\right)+1 = \frac{4\left(4^m-1\right)}{3}+1\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Next, we can use 3 as the common denominator and write 1 as \(\frac{3}{3}\) to get
      \begin{equation*} \frac{4\left(4^m-1\right)}{3}+1 = \frac{4\left(4^m-1\right)}{3}+\frac{3}{3} = \frac{4\left(4^m-1\right)+3}{3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Next, using the distributive property and simplifying we get
      \begin{equation*} \frac{4\left(4^m-1\right)+3}{3} = \frac{4\left(4^m\right)-4(1)+3}{3} =\frac{4\left(4^m\right)-1}{3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Last, we can simplify the power of 4 to get
      \begin{equation*} \frac{4\left(4^m\right)-1}{3} = \frac{4^{m+1}-1}{3} \end{equation*}

Definition 3.2.11. Complete graphs and complete bipartite graphs.

 
  1. A family of graphs is a set of graphs that share many properties. We might define a family in terms of a parameter such as \(s\text{.}\) In the name of the family, we often write that parameter as a subscript, a character written slightly smaller and lower. For example, the family of complete graphs \(K_s\) (pronounced “\(K\) sub \(s\)” ) for \(n\ge1\) are the graphs \(K_1\text{,}\) \(K_2\text{,}\) \(K_3\text{,}\) ….
    🔗
    🔗
  2. For \(s \ge 1\) the complete graph (or handshake graph) with \(s\) vertices, denoted \(K_s\text{,}\) has an edge between every pair of vertices. The subscript \(s\) tells us the number of vertices. Note that “complete” starts with the sound of the letter “K”. For example, in Example 3.2.8 Item 1, we drew the complete graph \(K_6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  3. For integers \(s,t \ge 1\text{,}\) the complete bipartite graph with \(s\) vertices of the first type (perhaps colored black in the drawing) and \(t\) vertices of the second type (perhaps colored white in the drawing), denoted \(K_{s,t}\) has an edge between each vertex of the first type and each vertex of the second type (and no other edges). For example, in Example 3.2.8 Item 2, we drew the complete bipartite graph \(K_{6,3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  4. A special case of complete bipartite graphs is the star graphs \(K_{1,t}\) for \(t\ge1\text{.}\) For example, a star graph might model a corporate reporting structure such as a vice-president and their directors. Figure 3.2.12 shows drawings of \(K_{1,4}\) and \(K_{1,5}\) where we have drawn the white vertices in a circle around the central black hub vertex, as is customary.

    Example 9.1.2. Counting \(\times 5\) squares.

    How many \(5 \times 5\) squares are there on the \(8 \times 8\) chessboard?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Think of taking a \(5 \times 5\) square and moving it around on top of the \(8 \times 8\) square. If we start with a the \(5 \times 5\) square in the upper left hand corner, we can move it one, two, or three units to the right. That is, there are four \(5 \times 5\) squares that fit along the top row. If we move it down one unit, there are also four \(5 \times 5\) squares that fit one row down. Similarly, there are four \(5 \times 5\) squares that fit two rows down and four \(5 \times 5\) squares that fit three rows down. That is the furthest we can go, so there are \(4+4+4+4=16\) different \(5 \times 5\) squares.
    🔗
    There is a more sophisticated way to count. We can create a pairing between each possible \(5 \times 5\) squares and its upper left-hand corner, which is a \(1 \times 1\) square. Which \(1 \times 1\) squares can be the upper left-hand corner of a \(5 \times 5\) square? The answer is any \(1 \times 1\) squares in the \(4 \times 4\) square that sits in the upper left-hand corner of the \(8 \times 8\) chessboard. (If we try to put the upper left-hand corner of the \(5 \times 5\) square anywhere else on the chessboard, then the \(5 \times 5\) square will not fit.) Because we have a pairing, the number of \(5 \times 5\) squares equals the number of \(1 \times 1\) squares in the \(4 \times 4\) square that sits in the upper left-hand corner of the \(8 \times 8\) chessboard. Since any \(4 \times 4\) square has exactly \(4^2=16\) of the \(1 \times 1\) squares, the answer again is \(4^2=16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

      Solution.

      \begin{equation*} 5! = \underline{1} \cdot 5! = \underline{1} \cdot 5! + \underline{0} \cdot 4! + \underline{0} \cdot 3! + \underline{0} \cdot 2! + \underline{0} \cdot 1! =(10000)_! \end{equation*}

      🔗
      🔗

    3. What is the largest multiple of \(4!\) you could have in the factorial representation? Explain. Generalize

      🔗
      🔗
      🔗

    We can now state the definition of the factorial representation.
    🔗

    Definition 9.1.5. Factorial representation.

    For a positive integer \(n\text{,}\) the factorial representation of \(n\) is
    \begin{equation*} n= (a_ka_{k-1}\dots a_3a_2a_1)_! \end{equation*}
    if
    \begin{equation*} n = a_kk!+a_{k-1}(k-1)! + \ldots a_33! + a_22! + a_11! \end{equation*}
    for some integer \(k\) where each integer \(a_i=1, 2, \ldots, i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.1.C Sum and Product Notation

    In Activity 9.1.A and Activity 9.1.B, we saw the sums \(1^2+3^2+3^2+ \ldots + 8^2\) and
    \begin{equation*} 1 \cdot 1! + 2 \cdot 2! + \ldots + (k-1) \cdot (k-1)! +k \cdot k! \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In our work with exponentials and factorials, we saw the products
    \begin{equation*} 2^n = \underbrace{2 \cdot 2 \cdot \ldots \cdot 2}_{n \text{ times } }\text{.} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} n! = (n)(n-1)\cdots(3)(2)(1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There is a standard notation for sums and products.
    🔗

    Definition 9.1.6. Sum and product notation.

    Consider a (finite) list of numbers
    \begin{equation*} a_s, a_{s+1}, a_{s+2}, \ldots, a_n \end{equation*}
    for some integers \(s\) and \(n\text{.}\)
    1. We can abbreviate their sum using summation (Sigma) notation:
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=s}^n a_k =a_s + a_{s+1}+a_{s+2}+\ldots+a_n \end{equation*}
      which is pronounced “the sum from \(s\) to \(n\) of \(a\) sub \(k\)”. For example, our answer to Activity 9.1.A is
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^8 k^2 =1^2+2^2+3^2+ \ldots + 8^2 = 204\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We can abbreviate their product using product (Pi) notation:
      \begin{equation*} \prod_{k=s}^n a_k =a_s \cdot a_{s+1} \cdot a_{s+2} \cdot \cdots \cdot a_n \end{equation*}
      which is pronounced “the product from \(s\) to \(n\) of \(a\) sub \(k\)”. For example,
      \begin{equation*} \prod_{k=1}^5 k =1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 5! = 120\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The numbers we add in a sum are the terms and the numbers we multiply in a product are the factors.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. We say the sum or product starts at \(s\text{.}\) Often \(s=0\) or \(s=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    By the way, the Greek letters \(\Sigma\) (sigma) and \(\Pi\) (pi) are the analogs of the letters S and P, which are the first letters of sum and product, respectively.
    🔗
    Let’s practice evaluating a sum and product.
    🔗

    Example 9.1.7. Summation and product notation.

     
    1. Evaluate \(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^4 (-1)^kk\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      This sum starts at \(k=1\) and ends when \(k=4\text{.}\) When \(k=1\text{,}\) we have \((-1)^kk=(-1)^11=-1\text{,}\) when \(k=2\text{,}\) we have \((-1)^kk=(-1)^22=2\text{,}\) when \(k=3\text{,}\) we have \((-1)^kk=(-1)^33=-3\text{,}\) and when \(k=4\text{,}\) we have \((-1)^kk=(-1)^44=4\text{.}\) We add these terms to get the sum
      \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^4 (-1)^kk =\underbrace{(-1)^1\cdot 1}_{k=1}+ \underbrace{(-1)^2\cdot 2}_{k=2}+ \underbrace{(-1)^3\cdot 3}_{k=3}+ \underbrace{(-1)^4\cdot 4}_{k=4} =-1 + 2 - 3 + 4 = 2 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Evaluate \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=0}^2(5k+1)\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      This product starts at \(k=0\) and ends when \(k=4\text{.}\) When \(k=0\text{,}\) we have \(5k+1=5(0)+1= 1\text{,}\) when \(k=1\text{,}\) we have \(5k+1=5(1)+1 = 6\text{,}\) and when \(k=2\text{,}\) we have \(5k+1=5(2)+1 = 11\text{.}\) We multiply these factors to get the product
      \begin{equation*} \prod_{k=0}^2(5k+1) = \underbrace{1}_{k=0} \cdot \underbrace{6}_{k=1} \cdot \underbrace{11}_{k=2} = 66\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write \(1 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + (n-1)\) using summation notation in a natural way starting at \(k=1\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      Our sum starts at 1 and ends at \(n-1\text{.}\) The terms equal the index so we get
      \begin{equation*} 1 + 2 + 3 + \ldots + (n-1) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Recall that the binomial coefficients \(\binom{n}{k}\) is the number of \(k\)-element subsets of a set of \(n\) elements. For example,
      \begin{equation*} \binom{4}{0} = 1 \binom{4}{1} = 4 \binom{4}{2} = 6 \binom{4}{3} = 4 \binom{4}{4} = 1 \end{equation*}

      \begin{align*} \sum_{k=0}^4 (-1)^4\binom{4}{k} \amp = (-1)^0\binom{4}{0} + (-1)^1\binom{4}{1} + (-1)^2\binom{4}{2} + (-1)^3\binom{4}{3} + (-1)^4\binom{4}{4}\\ \amp = 1-4+6-4+1=0\text{.} \end{align*}

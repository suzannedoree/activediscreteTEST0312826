    Section 5.4 Converse and Contrapositive

    In this section, we continue our discussion of conditional statements, including the converse and contrapositive of a conditional statement. The contrapositive provides an alternative proof format for proving that a conditional is true. We also introduce biconditional (if and only if) statements and the proof format for biconditional statements.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.1 \subconv

    The statement \(P \to Q\) promises that if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is also true. It does not say the reverse. The “reverse” might be one of two statements, each of which has a name.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.1. Converse and inverse.

     
    1. For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) the converse of \(P \to Q\) is \(Q \to P\text{.}\) For example, for the statement For all integers \(s\text{,}\) if \(s\) is even, then \(s^2\) is even. the converse is For all integers \(s\text{,}\) if \(s^2\) is even, then \(s\) is even.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In this context, we can translate the conditional \(P \to Q\) as “\(Q\) if \(P\)” and we can translate the converse \(Q \to P\) as “\(Q\) only if \(P\)”.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) the inverse of \(P \to Q\) is \(\neg P \to \neg Q\text{.}\) For example, for the statement For all integers \(m\text{,}\) if \(m\) is even, then \(m^2\) is even. the inverse is For all integers \(m\text{,}\) if \(m\) is odd, then \(m^2\) is odd.
      🔗
      🔗

    When both a conditional statement, \(P \to Q\text{,}\) and its converse, \(Q \to P\text{,}\) are true, we say “\(P\) if and only if \(Q\)”. We give a formal definition of the biconditional.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.4. Biconditional statements.

     
    1. For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) the biconditional statement, denoted \(P \leftrightarrow Q\text{,}\) is the following compound statement:
      \begin{equation*} (P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      For example, we saw in Example 5.4.2 Item 3 that if \(P\text{:}\) \(n\) is odd and \(Q\text{:}\) \(n+1\) is even, then \(P \to Q\) and \(Q \to P\) are true, so the biconditional statement \(P \leftrightarrow Q\) is also true.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The biconditional \(P \leftrightarrow Q\) is often translated as \(P\) if and only if \(Q\) and may also be abbreviated \(P\) iff \(Q\text{.}\) For example, when \(P\text{:}\) \(n\) is odd and \(Q\text{:}\) \(n+1\) is even, the biconditional \(P \leftrightarrow Q\) says For any integer \(n\text{,}\) \(n\) is odd if and only if \(n+1\) is even.

    Subsection 5.4.2 \subpffiff

    Since the biconditional is defined as
    \begin{equation*} P \leftrightarrow Q \equiv (P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)\text{,} \end{equation*}
    we can combine the proof formats for “and” [cross-reference to target(s) "pff_and" missing or not unique] with the proof format for a conditional statement [cross-reference to target(s) "pff_if_then_direct" missing or not unique] to get a new proof format to prove a biconditional statement. Normally in “and” proofs we begin the two parts with the words “First” and “Second” (or “First” and “Next”). In the proof of a biconditional statement, the second part proves the converse, so we begin with the word “Conversely” instead.
    🔗

    Proof.
      Prove: \(P \leftrightarrow Q\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    First, assume \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Explain why \(Q\) is true.)
    🔗
    Thus, \(Q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Conversely, assume \(Q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Explain why \(P\) is true.)
    🔗
    Thus, \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.4.7. Fractions and Divisibility.

    Let \(d\) and \(n\) be integers with \(d \neq 0\text{.}\) Prove that \(d \mid n\) exactly when \(\frac{n}{d}\) is an integer.
    🔗
    Proof. Let \(d\) and \(n\) be integers with \(d \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First, assume \(d \mid n\text{.}\) By definition of divides, \(n=dk\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) By 4.1.11, it follows that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n}{d} = \frac{dk}{d(1)} = \frac{k}{1} = k \end{equation*}
    is an integer.
    🔗
    Conversely, assume \(\frac{n}{d}\) is an integer. Say,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n}{d} = k = \frac{k}{1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    By 4.1.11, it follows that \(n(1) = k(d)\) or, equivalently, \(n=dk\text{.}\) By the definition of divides, \(d \mid n\text{.}\) \(\Box\)

    Subsection 5.4.3 \subcp

    While a conditional statement and its converse are not logically equivalent, there is a related conditional that is logically equivalent to a conditional.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.8. Contrapositive.

    For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) the contrapositive of the conditional statement \(P \to Q\) is the conditional statement \(\neg Q \to \neg P\text{.}\) For example, consider the true statement If \(k=3\text{,}\) then \(k^2=9\text{.}\) then the contrapositive is If \(k^2 \neq 9\text{,}\) then \(k \neq 3\text{.}\) which is also true.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s explore the logical equivalence of a conditional and its contrapositive.
    🔗

      Solution.
      The truth table is shown in Table 5.4.10. Notice that the columns for the conditional and its contrapositive are identical. That means that a conditional and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Explain in words why the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original conditional.
      Solution.
      Think about what makes the conditional \(P \to Q\) is true. We are promising that if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is true. What happens if \(\neg Q\) is true? That means \(Q\) is false and so \(P\) cannot be true (or we would break our promise) and so \(P\) must be false which means \(\neg P\) is true. That is, if \(\neg Q\text{,}\) then \(\neg P\text{,}\) which is the contrapositive.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We add the contrapositive to our list of logical equivalences.

    Theorem 5.4.11. Contraposition equivalence.

    For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} (P \to Q) \equiv (\neg Q \to \neg P) \end{equation*}

    Activity 5.4.4. Contrapositive proof if \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is also odd.

     
    1. Perhaps you noticed in Activity 5.4.3 Item 4 that the contrapositive would be easier to prove. Copy the following proof. Prove for any integer \(n\text{,}\) if \(n^2\) is odd then \(n\) is odd.
      Proof.
      Let \(n\) be an integer. We will prove the contrapositive, namely that if \(n\) is even, then \(n^2\) is even.
      🔗
      Assume that \(n\) is even. By the definition of even, we can write \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} n^2=(2k)^2=(2k)(2k) = (2)(2)kk = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Since \(2k^2\) is an integer, it follows from the definition of even that \(n^2\) is even.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗

    Example 5.4.12. Rewriting a proof by contradiction using the contrapositive.

     
    1. Read the following proof by contradiction. Prove that for any integers \(a\) and \(b\) if \(ab\) is even, then \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even.
      Proof.
      Suppose not. Then for some integers \(a\) and \(b\) we would have that \(ab\) is even but neither \(a\) nor \(b\) is even.
      🔗
      First, since neither \(a\) nor \(b\) is even, that means \(a\) and \(b\) are odd. By the definition of odd, we can write \(a = 2k_1+1\) and \(b = 2k_2+1\) for some integers \(k_1\) and \(k_2\text{.}\) Multiplying \(a\) and \(b\) we get
      \begin{equation*} ab=(2k_1+1)(2k_2+1)=4k_1k_2+2k_1+2k_2+1 = 2(2k_1k_2 + k_1 + k_2) + 1 \end{equation*}
      and so \(ab\) is odd.
      🔗
      On the other hand, we said \(ab\) was even. This is a contradiction.
      🔗
      🔗
      What are the contradictory statements \(Q\) and \(\neg Q\text{?}\)
      Solution.
      The contradictory statements are \(Q: ab\) is odd and \(\neg Q: ab\) is even.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. State the contrapositive of the statement “For any integers \(a\) and \(b\) if \(ab\) is even, then \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even.”
      Solution.
      By DeMorgan’s Laws Theorem 5.1.16, the negation of the conclusion \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even. is the statement \(a\) and \(b\) are odd. The contrapositive is For any integers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) if \(a\) and \(b\) are odd, then \(ab\) is odd. Notice that we keep the universal quantifier.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Adapt the proof from Item 1 to prove for any integers \(a\) and \(b\) if \(ab\) is even, then either \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even using the contrapositive [cross-reference to target(s) "pff_if_then_cp" missing or not unique]. Hint: you should not have to change much of the proof.
      Solution.
      Prove: for any integers \(a\) and \(b\) if \(ab\) is even, then either \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even.
      🔗
      Proof. We will prove the contrapositive, namely for any integers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) if \(a\) and \(b\) are odd, then \(ab\) is odd.
      🔗
      Let \(a\) and \(b\) be odd integers. By the definition of odd, we can write \(a = 2k_1+1\) and \(b = 2k_2+1\) for some integers \(k_1\) and \(k_2\text{.}\) Multiplying \(a\) and \(b\) we get

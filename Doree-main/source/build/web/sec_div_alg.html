    Section 4.2 The Division Algorithm

    In this section, we continue our discussion of integer division. We introduce vocabulary to describe the result of dividing one integer by another when the answer is not an integer. Next, we revisit polynomials and use polynomials to prove facts about even, odd, and divisors using cases.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.1 \subdivalg

    As we saw in Section 4.1, sometimes when we divide one integer by another integer we get an integer answer, such as
    \begin{equation*} 40 \div 5 = \frac{40}{5} = 8\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We said that 5 divides 40 and wrote \(5 \mid 40\text{.}\) Other times, we get a fraction that is not an integer, such as
    \begin{equation*} 23 \div 4= \frac{23}{4} = 5.75\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We said that 4 does not divide 23 and wrote \(4 \nmid 23\text{.}\) How can we think of \(23 \div 4\) in terms of integers?
    🔗

    Example 4.2.1. Cookies and Children.

    Use the idea of giving 23 cookies to four children to describe \(23 \div 4\) in terms of (only) integers.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Imagine that I have 23 cookies and four children. I want to give each child as many cookies as I can, but, to be fair, each child should have the same number of cookies as the other children. What should I do? I can give each child five cookies. That accounts for \(5 \times 4 = 20\) of the cookies, but I cannot evenly distribute the remaining \(23-20=3\) cookies without breaking them, which I do not want to do. I guess those 3 cookies are for me to eat. We might say \(23 \div 4\) is 5 cookies/child with 3 cookies remaining for me. We can write the equation
    \begin{equation*} 23 = 5 \times 4 + 3\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We formalize the idea from Example 4.2.1 with the following theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.2.2. The Division Algorithm.

    Given integers \(a\) and \(n\) with \(n \ge 1\text{,}\) when we divide \(a\) by \(n\) we get
    \begin{equation*} a = qn + r \end{equation*}
    for some integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that \(0 \le r \lt n\text{.}\) Moreover, \(q\) and \(r\) are unique.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(a\) and \(n\) be integers with \(n \ge 1\text{.}\) We prove existence in the case where \(a \ge 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(a\) is nonnegative and \(n \ge 1\text{,}\) we can imagine giving \(a\) cookies to \(n\) children. First, I check to see if I have enough cookies for each child to get at least one, and if so, then I hand one cookie to each child. Next, I repeat that step with the remaining cookies as many times as possible. In the end, let’s say that each child gets \(q\) cookies. In total, I gave \(q \times n=qn\) cookies to the children, so there are
    \begin{equation*} a-qn=r \end{equation*}
    cookies leftover for me. Adding \(qn\) to each side of the equation we get
    \begin{equation*} a = qn+r\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Because I checked there were enough cookies for each child to get one, I never run out of cookies, and so \(r \ge 0\text{.}\) On the other hand, any time I still have enough cookies to give each child another cookie, I would do that. Therefore, the final number of remaining cookies must be strictly less than the number of children and so \(r \lt n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The integers \(q\) and \(r\) in the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 have special names.
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.3. Quotient, Remainder, \fn{div}, and \fn{mod}.

    Let \(a\) and \(n\) be integers with \(n \ge 1\) and let \(q\) and \(r\) be integers such that \(a = qn+r\) and \(0 \le r \lt n\text{.}\)
    1. The integer \(q\) is the quotient of \(a \div n\text{.}\) When \(a \ge 0\text{,}\) it is the number of cookies per child in the story. For example, the quotient when \(23 \div 4\) is five.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We write \(a\) div \(n =q\text{.}\) For example, \(23\) div \(4=5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The integer \(r\) is the remainder of \(a \div n\text{.}\) When \(a \ge 0\text{,}\) it is the number of cookies for me. For example, the remainder when \(23 \div 4\) is three.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. We write \(a\) mod \(n=r\text{.}\) For example, \(23\) mod \(4=3\text{.}\) In many computer programming languages, mod is denoted using the percent symbol (%). For example, we would write \(23 \% 4 = 3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. We say \(a \div n\) is \(q\) with remainder \(r\text{.}\) For example, \(23 \div 4\) is five with remainder three.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. In long division the quotient is typically written at the top of the bar and the remainder is the final number at the bottom, as in Figure 4.2.4.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s see these definitions in action.
    🔗

    Example 4.2.5. Quotient, Remainder, \fn{div}, and \fn{mod}.

    On this problem we do not use computational technology (except to check).
    1. Find the quotient and the remainder when \(100 \div 9\text{.}\) Use your answer to calculate \(100\) div \(9\) and \(100\) mod \(9\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      Starting with 100 cookies, we recognize that \(9 \times 11 = 99\) and so we can hand each child 11 cookies, leaving us with \(100 - (11\times 9) = 100-99 = 1\) cookie.
      🔗
      If we were using computational technology, we could calculate \(100 \div 9 = 11.1111.\text{..}\) and again see that 11 is the largest number of cookies we can give each child, as there are not enough cookies to give each child 12 cookies.
      🔗
      If we did a long division instead, we would get the same answer, as shown in Figure 4.2.4.
      🔗
      Notice that in the long division we calculate the remainder in two steps. First, we give each child 10 cookies for a total of \(10 \times 9 = 90\) cookies (although 0 is not written), so the remainder is \(100-90 = 10\text{.}\) Next, we give each child 1 cookie for a total of \(1 \times 9 = 9\) cookies, so the final remainder is \(10-9=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Using any way of thinking of this division, we get
      \begin{equation*} 100 = 11(9) +1\text{,} \end{equation*}
      so the quotient is 11 and the remainder is 1. Thus, \(100\) div \(9=11\) and \(100\) mod \(9=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the quotient and the remainder when \(35 \div 5\text{.}\) Use your answer to calculate \(35\) div \(5\) and \(35\) mod \(5\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      Write \(35 = 7(5)\) so the quotient is seven. To find the remainder, think of the equation as \(35 = 7(5)+0\text{,}\) so the remainder is zero. Thus, \(35\) div \(5=7\) and \(35\) mod \(5=0\)
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use the mod notation (or %) to describe whether an integer is even or odd.
      Solution.
      An integer \(n\) is even if \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) When we divide, we get \(n \div 2 = k\) and the remainder is zero. Thus, \(n\) is even exactly when \(n\) mod \(2=0\) or, equivalently, when \(n\%2=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      An integer \(n\) is odd if \(n=2k+1\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) When we divide, we get \(n \div 2 = k\text{,}\) and the remainder is one. Thus, \(n\) is even exactly when \(n\) mod \(2=1\) or, equivalently, when \(n\%2=1\text{.}\)

    Subsection 4.2.2 \subpolys

    The Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 gives us expressions such as \(2k+1\) for an odd integer or \(3q+2\) for an integer where that integer \(\fn{mod}~ 3 = 2\text{.}\) We might square an even integer to get an expression of the form \(4n^2\text{.}\) These expressions are polynomials. In this section, we take a look at polynomials, including how to simplify products and powers. We start with the definition.
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.7. Polynomials.

     
    1. For an integer \(n\text{,}\) a polynomial in \(n\) is a sum of constant multiples of powers of \(n\text{.}\) For example, \(2m^4+5m^2-m+7\) is a polynomial in \(m\text{.}\) To see why, we can rewrite this polynomial to show the powers of \(n\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation*} 2m^4+5m^2-m+7=2(m^4)+5(m^2)+(-1)(m^1)+7(m^0)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In a polynomial, the integer in front of a power of \(n\) is the coefficient of that power of \(n\text{.}\) For example, in the polynomial \(2m^3+5m^2-m+7\text{,}\) the coefficient of \(m^4\) is 2, the coefficient of \(m^2\) is 5, the coefficient of \(m\) is \(-1\text{,}\) and the coefficient of \(m^3\) is 0.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. In a polynomial, the coefficient of \(m^0\) is the constant term. For example, the constant term of \(2m^3+5m^2-m+7\) is 7.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. We add polynomials by collecting like terms. For example
      \begin{equation*} (k^3 + 2k -7) + (4k^3 + k^2 + 2) = (k^3+4k^3) + (k^2) + (2k) + (-7 +2) = 5k^3+k^2+2k-5 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. To multiply or expand a product of polynomials, we use the distributive rule. See, for example, Example 4.2.8.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We use the distributive rule Definition 2.1.17 to expand polynomials.
    🔗

    Example 4.2.8. Multiplying polynomials.

     
    1. Expand \(n^2(2n+1)\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      By the distributive rule,
      \begin{equation*} n^2(2n+1) = n^2(2n) + n^2(1) = 2n^3+n^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Expand \((n+2)(n+3)\text{.}\)
      Solution.
      By the distributive rule (repeatedly),
      🔗
      \((n+3)(n+2)\) = \(n(n+2) + 3(n+2)\)
      = \(\underbrace{(n)(n)}_{\text{ First } } +\underbrace{(n)(2)}_{\text{ Outside } } + \underbrace{(3)(n)}_{\text{ Inside } } + \underbrace{(3)(2)}_{\text{ Last } }\)
      = \(n^2 + 2n + 3n + 6\)
      = \(n^2+5n + 6\)
      The words under each product indicate the original location of the two integers that are multiplied. This method is First, Outside, Inside, Last or FOIL.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2.3 \subpffcases

    We can use the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 to prove that every integer is even or odd, but never both.
    🔗

    Example 4.2.9. Every integer is even or odd.

    Use the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 to prove that every integer is even or odd.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Proof Let \(a\) be an integer. By the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 with \(n=2\text{,}\) there exist unique integers \(q\) and \(r\) such that \(a=qn+r\) and \(0 \le r \lt 2\text{.}\) Note that \(r=0\) or \(r=1\text{.}\) Since \(r\) is unique, we are in exactly one of these two cases. Case 1: Assume \(r=0\text{.}\) Then \(a = 2q+0 = 2q\) is even, by the definition of even. Case 2: Assume \(r=1\text{.}\) Then \(a=2q+1\) is odd, by the definition of odd. In either case \(a\) is even or \(a\) is odd.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As in Example 4.2.9, the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2 can be used to break a proof into cases, similar to how we break counting arguments into cases in Section 2.2. Here is the proof format.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Prove: \(P\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    (Explain why there are \(n\) cases.) We consider \(n\) cases.
    🔗
    Case 1: Assume … (Explain why \(P\) is true in this case.)
    🔗
    Case 2: Assume … (Explain why \(P\) is true in this case.)
    🔗
    …
    🔗
    Case \(n\text{:}\) Assume … (Explain why \(P\) is true in this case.)
    🔗
    In (either case/all cases) we have \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In a proof by cases, it is important that the cases cover all of the possibilities. Sometimes we need to explain that up front. The phrase “We consider \(n\) cases” that alerts the reader that we are using a proof by cases. The assumption for each case “Assume that …”. is stated as a complete sentence (with a period at the end) before any additional argument is presented. After we finish the cases, we summarize using the phrase “in either case”. If there are more than two cases, we use “in all cases”.
    🔗

    Activity 4.2.3. Proof by Cases: \(n^2 + n\) is even.

     
    1. Copy the following example of a proof by cases. Prove that \(n^2+n\) is even for any integer \(n\text{.}\)
      Proof.
      Let \(n\) be an integer. By 4.2.9, \(n\) is even or \(n\) is odd. We consider two cases.
      🔗
      Case 1: Assume \(n\) is even. By the definition of even, \(n = 2k\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) Thus
      \begin{equation*} n^2+n=(2k)^2+(2k) = 4k^2+2k = 2(2k^2+k)\text{,} \end{equation*}
      so \(n^2+n\) is even.
      🔗
      Case 2: Assume \(n\) is odd. By the definition of odd, \(n=2k+1\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) Thus
      \begin{equation*} n^2+n= (2k+1)^2 +(2k+1) = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 + 2k + 1 = 4k^2 + 6k + 2 = 2(2k^2 + 3k + 1)\text{,} \end{equation*}
      so \(n^2+n\) is even.
      🔗
      In either case, \(n^2+n\) is even.
      🔗
      🔗

    Example 4.2.10. Using proof by cases to prove multiple of three.

    Use a proof by cases to prove
    \begin{equation*} 3 \mid n(n+1)(n+2) \end{equation*}
    for any integer \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(n\) be an integer. By the Division Algorithm Theorem 4.2.2, we can write
    \begin{equation*} n=3q+r \end{equation*}
    for some integers \(q\) and \(r\) with \(0 \le r \lt 3\text{.}\) The only possible values of \(r\) are \(r=0\text{,}\) \(r=1\text{,}\) and \(r=2\text{.}\) We consider three cases.
    🔗
    Case 1: Assume \(r=0\text{.}\) In this case, \(n=3q\) and so
    \begin{equation*} n(n+1)(n+2)= (3q)(3q+1)(3q+2) = 3[q(3q+1)(3q+2)] = 3k \end{equation*}
    where \(k = q(3q+1)(3q+2)\text{.}\) Thus, \(3\mid n(n+1)(n+2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 2: Assume \(r=1\text{.}\) In this case, \(n=3q+1\) and so
    \begin{equation*} n(n+1)(n+2)= (3q+1)(3q+2)(3q+3) = (3q+1)(3q+2)3(q+1) =3[(3q+1)(3q+2)(q+1)] = 3k \end{equation*}
    where \(k = (3q+1)(3q+2)(q+1)\text{.}\) Thus, \(3\mid n(n+1)(n+2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 3: Assume \(r=2\text{.}\) In this case, \(n=3q+2\) and so
    \begin{equation*} n(n+1)(n+2)= (3q+2)(3q+3)(3q+4) = (3q+2)3(q+1)(3q+4) = 3[(3q+2)(q+1)(3q+4)] = 3k \end{equation*}
    where \(k = (3q+2)(q+1)(3q+4)\text{.}\) Thus, \(3\mid n(n+1)(n+2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In all cases, \(3\mid n(n+1)(n+2)\text{.}\)

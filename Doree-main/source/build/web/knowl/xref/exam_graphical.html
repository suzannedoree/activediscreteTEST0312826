Example 2.2.6. Is this list a degree sequence of a graph?

For each list of nonnegative integers, draw a graph with that degree sequence or explain why there cannot be a graph with that degree sequence.
  1. \(5, 5, 3, 3, 3, 3\)
    Solution.
    Let’s see what happens if we can draw a graph with this degree sequence. Note that there are six numbers listed, so our graph has six vertices. Therefore, each of the degree five vertices must be adjacent to all of the other five vertices. Therefore, our graph must start as shown on the left in Figure 2.2.7. The vertices \(v_2\) and \(w_2\) have degree five, but the other vertices only have degree two. We can increase their degree to three, as desired, by adding the edges \(\{v_1,w_1\}\) and \(\{v_3,w_3\}\text{,}\) for example, obtaining the graph drawn on the right in Figure 2.2.7. Thus, this list is the degree sequence of a graph.
    🔗
    Note that this graph is not the only graph with this degree sequence. We could have drawn edges between \(v_1\) and \(v_3\) and between \(w_1\) and \(w_3\text{,}\) for example.

  2. \(5, 5, 3, 2, 2, 1\)
    Solution.
    Let’s see if we can draw a graph with this degree sequence. It would have six vertices. As before, the two vertices of degree five would each be adjacent to the other four vertices, as in the graph drawn on the left in Figure 2.2.7. But then all of the other vertices in our graph would already have degree at least two. That is a problem because our degree sequence lists a vertex of degree one. Thus, this list is not the degree sequence of any graph.

Example 2.1.5. Counting squares and dominoes tilings of a \(1 \times 8\) board.

 
  1. Based on the recursive solution to tile a \(1 \times n\) board using squares and dominoes in Example 2.1.2, how many ways can we tile a \(1 \times 8\) board?
    Solution.
    We saw in Example 2.1.2 that there are \(8+5=13\) ways to tile a \(1 \times 6\) board: eight are obtained by putting a square in front of each tiling of a \(1 \times 5\) board and five are obtained by putting a domino in front of each tiling of a \(1 \times 4\) board.
    🔗
    Similarly, there are \(13+8=21\) ways to tile a \(1 \times 7\) board: 13 are obtained by placing a square in front of each tiling of a \(1 \times 6\) board and eight are obtained by placing a domino in front of each tiling of a \(1 \times 5\) board.
    🔗
    Continuing this reasoning, there are \(21+13 = 34\) tilings of a \(1 \times 8\) board.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Conjecture the number of ways to tile a \(1 \times n\) board.
    Solution.
    The one-shorter board is \(1 \times (n-1)\) and the two-shorter board is \(1 \times (n-2)\text{.}\) Since we got one tiling of the \(1 \times n\) board for each tiling of a \(1 \times (n-1)\) board and for each tiling of a \(1 \times (n-2)\) board, the number of tilings of a \(1 \times n\) equals the sum of the number of tilings of a \(1 \times (n-1)\) board and the number of tilings of a \(1 \times (n-2)\) board. That last sentence is a mouthful. Using sequence notation, which we introduce formally in [cross-reference to target(s) "sec_explicitly_defined_sequences" missing or not unique], we might write
    \begin{equation*} b_n=b_{n-1}+b_{n-2} \end{equation*}
    where the subscript on \(b_k\) represents the number of ways to tile a \(1 \times k\) board.

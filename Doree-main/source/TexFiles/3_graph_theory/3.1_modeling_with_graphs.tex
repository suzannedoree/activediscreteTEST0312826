We begin with a puzzle.  

\begin{act}[Bridges Puzzle]  %% SU:  should say Bank not Shore!
\label{act_bridges}   
   A small city sits on the north and south shores of a river and on two islands on the river, a small island and a big island, as shown in \Cref{fig_bridges}. Residents enjoy walking around the city each day, crossing the bridges that connect the islands to each other and to the north and south shore. To keep their walks interesting, they never cross the same bridge twice in a day.

        \begin{figure}[hbtp]
            \centering
            \input{Tikz/bridges}
            %\includegraphics[scale=.6]{Images/Bridges.png}{}  %% Handcaptured png file
            \caption{A small city connected by bridges.}
            \label{fig_bridges}
        \end{figure}
    
        \begin{actpart}
        
        \item Show that it is possible to start a daily walk on the small island and cross every bridge (exactly once each).  You end up somewhere other than the small island (with no way back to where you started).  Where do you end?  Any thoughts on why you end there?
        \item Why is it impossible to start a daily walk on the small island, cross every bridge (exactly once each), and end on the small island?  Hint: There are three bridges connected to the small island. The first bridge you cross takes you off the small island.  At some point you cross a second bridge connected to the small island.  Where are you then?  Where are you after crossing the third bridge connected to the small island?  
        \item What happens if you start a daily walk somewhere else (not on small island) and cross every bridge connected to small island (exactly once each)? Hint: Whichever of the bridges connected to small island that you cross first takes you onto small island.  Where are you after the next bridge you cross?  At some point you cross the third bridge connected to the small island.  Where are you then?
        \item Explain why it is impossible to start a daily walk somewhere other than small island, cross every bridge (exactly once each), and end where you started.  
        \item Look at the number of bridges connected to each piece of land.  What about that number matters in trying to find a daily walk that crosses each bridge (exactly once each)?
    \end{actpart}
\end{act}

Let's examine the key elements of \Cref{act_bridges}.  In the puzzle, it did not matter where the north shore, the south shore, the small island, and the big island were geographically.  We did not care how long the bridges were or what angle they made with the shore or island.  It also did not matter that the small island is smaller than the big island.  We did not even care which pieces of land were islands and which were the shores of the river. All that mattered was which pieces of land had a bridge, or two, between them.

\newcommand{\subdefngraph}{The Definition of a Graph}
\subsection{\subdefngraph}
\label{sub_defn_graph}

In \Cref{act_bridges}, the essential information we needed to know about the city was the set of objects (the pieces of land) and the connections between them (the bridges).
Any set of objects with connections between pairs of objects is a \vocab{graph}. 

\begin{defn}[Graph]
\label{defn_graph} 
~
    \begin{apart}       
        \item A \vocab{vertex} (or \vocab{node}) is an object.  For example, a vertex could be a piece of land in the city from \Cref{act_bridges} or perhaps a student at the university.  The plural of ``vertex'' is ``\vocab{vertices}'' and so we refer to one vertex, two vertices, three vertices, and so on. 
        
        \item An \vocab{edge} is a set of two vertices.  For example, if $A$ and $B$ are distinct vertices in a graph, then $\{A,B\}$ is an example of a possible edge.  When we model a situation using a graph, each edge represents a connection between two objects. For example, in the Bridges Puzzle (\Cref{act_bridges}), we might want an edge between two vertices to indicate that those two pieces of land are connected by a bridge.\footnote{We are ignoring, for the moment, that there are two bridges between the big island and the north shore.  Technically we need a slightly more general definition of an edge and what we get is a \vocab{multigraph}, not a simple graph.} As another example, in a graph where each vertex is a student at the university, we might want an edge between two vertices to indicate that those two students have a class together this semester.  

        \item Two vertices are \vocab{adjacent} (or \vocab{neighbors}) if they are connected by an edge.  That is, if $A$ and $B$ are distinct vertices and $\{A,B\}$ is an edge, then the vertices $A$ and $B$ are adjacent.  We also say that $A$ is \vocab{adjacent to} $B$ or that $B$ is \vocab{adjacent to} $A$. So, for example, if Camilla and Quang are both in discrete mathematics class this semester, then Camilla is adjacent to Quang (and vice versa) in the student graph.
        
        \item \label{part_defn_graph} A \vocab{(simple) graph} (or \vocab{network}) is a pair of sets: a \vocab{vertex set} of vertices and a \vocab{edge set} of edges where the elements of each edge are in the vertex set.  For example, we might have the graph $G$ with the vertex set $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and the edge set $$\{\{1,2\},\{1,4\},\{2,3\},\{2,4\},\{3,4\}\}.$$
        
        \item A \vocab{graph drawing} of a graph has a point for each vertex  and a line (or curve) for each edge connecting its two vertices. In this textbook, we draw each vertex as a small, open circle instead of a point.  The placement of the vertices and edges is at our convenience-- none of the geometry such as position, length, or angle matters. For example, \Cref{fig_four_drawings_K4_minus_edge} shows four different drawings of the graph $G$ from (\ref{part_defn_graph}).  
  
        \begin{figure}[hbtp]
            \centering          
            \input{Tikz/K4_minus_edge}                
            \caption{Four different drawings of the graph $G$.}
            \label{fig_four_drawings_K4_minus_edge}
        \end{figure}            
    \end{apart}
\end{defn}

The city in the Bridges Puzzle can be modeled with a \vocab{multigraph} which is a graph where multiple edges are allowed between the same pair of vertices.

Let's look at an another example of a graph model.

\begin{exam}[Classmates graph]
\label{exam_classmates_graph}
    Abdi, Bhavika, Cara, Drew, Elle, and Filipe are all new transfer students. This semester, Abdi, Bhavika, and Cara are in a discrete mathematics class together; Bhavika, Cara, and Drew are in an algorithms class together; and Abdi and Elle are in chemistry class together.  These students do not have other classes in common. 
    
    We can model these relationships with a graph.  The vertex set $\{A,B,C,D,E,F\}$ has a vertex for each student corresponding to the first letter of their name.  
    Let's say two vertices are connected by an edge if those two students are taking the same class. If students have any number of classes together, we will just draw a single edge.  This graph might be useful for visualizing who knows each other.
    \begin{apart}
        \item Explain why $\{A,C\}$ is an edge but $\{B,E\}$ is not.
            \begin{soln}
                Abdi and Cara are in the discrete mathematics class, so $\{A,C\}$ is an edge.  All common classes are listed, and there is no class that Bhavika and Elle are taking, so $\{B,E\}$ is not an edge.
            \end{soln}
        \item How many edges are in the graph?
            \begin{soln}
                First, since Abdi, Bhavika, and Cara are in a discrete mathematics class together, we have three edges $\{A,B\}$, $\{A,C\}$, and $\{B,C\}$.
                Next, since Bhavika, Cara, and Drew are in an algorithms class, we have two more edges $\{B,D\}$ and $\{C,D\}$. We already knew that $\{B,C\}$ was an edge, so we do not draw that edge again.
                Lastly, since Abdi and Elle are in the same chemistry class, we have one more edge: $\{A,E\}$.  There are a total of six edges.
            \end{soln} 
        \item Draw the graph.
            \begin{soln}
                \Cref{fig_classmates_graph} shows one possible drawing of the graph.  In this drawing, we chose to draw the vertices in a circle.  The lines representing the edges $\{A,C\}$ and $\{B,D\}$ happen to cross, which is acceptable, but note that the point where those two edges cross is not a vertex. That is the sort of potential misunderstanding that we avoid by drawing our vertices as small circles instead of actual points.

                \begin{figure}[hbtp]
                    \centering
                    \input{Tikz/classmates_graph}
                    \caption{One drawing of the classmates graph.}
                    \label{fig_classmates_graph}
                \end{figure}            
            \end{soln}
    \end{apart}
\end{exam}

It is useful to have a name for the number of edges that include a particular vertex.

\begin{defn}[Degree of vertex]
\label{defn_degree}   
~
    \begin{apart}
        \item The \vocab{degree} of a vertex $v$ in a graph $G$, denoted $\fn{deg}_G(v)$ or just $\fn{deg}(v)$, is the number of edges of $G$ that contain the vertex $v$.  For example, in the graph $G$ from \Cref{defn_graph} (\ref{part_defn_graph}) with vertex set $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and edge set $$\{\{1,2\}, \{1,4\},\{2,3\},\{2,4\},\{3,4\}\},$$ vertex 1 has degree two because 1 is an element of two edges: $\{1,2\}$ and $\{1,4\}$.  Vertex 2 has degree three because 2 is an element of three edges: $\{1,2\}$, $\{2,3\}$, and $\{2,4\}$. In the drawings in \Cref{fig_four_drawings_K4_minus_edge}, we see two edges to vertex 1 and three edges to vertex 2.
        \item Equivalently, the \vocab{degree} of a vertex is the number of neighbors of the vertex. For example, in the graph $G$ from \Cref{defn_graph} (\ref{part_defn_graph}), vertex 1 has degree two because it has two neighbors, 2 and 4.  Similarly, vertex 2 has degree three because it has three neighbors, 1, 3, and 4.
        \item An \vocab{isolated vertex} is a vertex with degree zero.  For example, $F$ is an isolated vertex in the classmates graph in \Cref{exam_classmates_graph}, because Felipe was not a classmate of anyone else in the group of new transfer students. In \Cref{fig_classmates_graph} there are no edges to the vertex $F$.
        \item A \vocab{end vertex} (or \vocab{leaf} or \vocab{pendant}) is a vertex with degree 1. For example, in the classmates graph from \Cref{exam_classmates_graph}, $E$ is an end vertex because Elle was only in a class with Abdi. In \Cref{fig_classmates_graph} we see one edge to the vertex $E$.
    \end{apart}
\end{defn}

\newcommand{\subgraphcomp}{Graph Complements}
\subsection{\subgraphcomp}
\label{sub_graph_complement}

When we first introduced graphs in \Cref{sub_defn_graph}, we explained that in a graph, $G$, an edge connecting two vertices, $v$ and $w$, indicates that the two objects, $v$ and $w$, are related in some way.  For example, in \Cref{exam_classmates_graph}, the objects were a group of new transfer students and an edge between two vertices indicated that the two students were in a class together this semester.  That graph is helpful if we want to visualize who knows each other.  Suppose instead that we wanted to set up one-on-one meetings for new transfer students who do not yet know each other.  We might want to draw a new graph where the objects are the same group of new transfer students, but now an edge between two vertices indicates that the two students are not in a class together this semester.  This new graph, $\overline{G}$, has the same vertices as $G$, but now an edge connecting two vertices, $v$ and $w$, indicates that the two objects, $v$ and $w$ are related in the opposite way.

We formally define the graph complement.

\begin{defn}[Graph complement]
\label{defn_graph_complement}
    For a graph, $G$, the \vocab{(graph) complement}, $\overline{G}$, has the same set of vertices but opposite edges in the sense that $\{v,w\}$ is an edge of $\overline{G}$ exactly when $\{v,w\}$ is not an edge in $G$.  Equivalently, $v$ and $w$ are adjacent in $\overline{G}$ exactly when $v$ and $w$ are not adjacent in $G$.
\end{defn}

Let's draw the complement of the classmates graph.

\begin{exam}[Complement of the classmates graph]
\label{exam_classmates_comp}  
    Draw the complement of the classmates graph in \Cref{fig_classmates_graph}.
        \begin{soln}
            We draw the complement in \Cref{fig_classmates_comp}. 
            
                \begin{figure}[hbtp]
                    \centering
                    \input{Tikz/classmates_comp}
                    \caption{The complement of the classmates graph.}
                    \label{fig_classmates_comp}
                \end{figure}
                
            For example, $A$ and $D$ are not adjacent vertices in the classmates graph because Abdi and Drew are not in any of the same classes this semester.  Therefore, $A$ and $D$ are adjacent vertices in the complement of the classmates graph.  As another example, $B$ and $C$ are adjacent vertices in the classmates graph because Bhavika and Cara are in the algorithms class together this semester. Therefore, $B$ and $C$ are not adjacent vertices in the complement of the classmates graph.
        \end{soln}
\end{exam}

Let's work through \Cref{act_graph_comp} (\ref{part_deg_comp}).

\begin{exam}[Degree of a vertex in the complement]
\label{exam_deg_comp}
    In the graph $G$ drawn in \Cref{fig_graph_act_deg_comp}, what is the relationship between the degree of a vertex in the graph $G$ and the degree of that same vertex in the complement $\overline{G}$?
        \begin{soln}
            As an example, the vertex $v$ is adjacent to $w$, $x$, and $z$ in $G$ and $v$ is adjacent to $y$ in $\overline{G}$.  Thus, $\fn{deg}_G(v) = 3$ and $\fn{deg}_{\overline{G}}(v) = 1$. As another example, the vertex $z$ is adjacent to $v$ and $y$ in $G$ and $z$ is adjacent to $w$ and $x$ in $\overline{G}$. Thus, $\fn{deg}_G(z) = 2$ and $\fn{deg}_{\overline{G}}(z) = 2$. In general, if $x$ is any vertex in $G$, then $$\fn{deg}_G(x) +\fn{deg}_{\overline{G}}(x) = 4.$$
            
            This equation holds for any vertex, $x$, because there are four other vertices in $G$.  Some of those four vertices are adjacent to $x$ in $G$.  The remainder of those four vertices must be adjacent to $x$ in $\overline{G}$.  So, each of those other four vertices counts once in either $\fn{deg}_G(x)$ or $\fn{deg}_{\overline{G}}(x)$.
        \end{soln}
\end{exam}

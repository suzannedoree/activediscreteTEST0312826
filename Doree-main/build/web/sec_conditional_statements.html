    Section 5.3 Conditional Statements

    Most statements in mathematics and computer science involve conditionals: if one thing happens, then another thing happens. Consider, for example, a database that tracks patients who have scheduled follow-up visits (or not). We might want to send a reminder to patients who have not yet scheduled their follow-up visits. That is, if the patient has not scheduled their follow-up visit, then we want to send them a reminder.
    🔗
    In this section, we study conditional statements and how to prove that they are true. We also look at their negations and construct counterexamples.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.A Promises: Conditional Statements

    We begin with a definition.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.1. Conditional statements.

     
    1. For statements \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) the statement “if \(P\text{,}\) then \(Q\)”, denoted \(P \to Q\text{,}\) is a conditional statement. We also say \(P\) implies \(Q\text{.}\) The statement \(P \to Q\) promises that if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) must also be true. For example, consider the following statement: For any integer \(k\text{,}\) if \(k=3\text{,}\) then \(k^2=9\text{.}\) This statement is true because when \(k=3\) we have \(k^2 = 3^2 = 9\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In the statement \(P \to Q\text{,}\) the statement \(P\) is the hypothesis and \(Q\) is the conclusion. For example, consider the following statement: If a graph is a tree, then it has a leaf. The hypothesis is that a graph is a tree. The conclusion is that it has a leaf.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    For \(P \to Q\) to be true, it must be the case that if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is 100% guaranteed to also be true. It might be the case that \(P\) causes \(Q\text{,}\) for example, if \(P\) is the statement “Tova dropped her glove in a puddle” a nd \(Q\) is the statement “Tova’s glove got wet”, then \(P \to Q\) is the statement “If Tova dropped her glove in a puddle, then Tova’s glove got wet”, which is true. In this example, \(P\) caused \(Q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Alternatively, it might be the case that \(Q\) had to happen in order for \(P\) to happen. For example, if \(P\) is the statement “Derek got an A in Algorithms class” and \(Q\) is the statement “Derek took the Algorithms class” then \(P \to Q\) is the statement “if Derek got an A in Algorithms class, then Derek took the Algorithms class” which is true. In this example, \(Q\) would have to happen before \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let’s look more closely at when a conditional is true or false.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.2. Understanding when a conditional is true.

    Consider the following conditional statement \(S\) which is “If you show up to class every day and do all of the homework on time, then you will get an A in this class.”
    1. Is \(S\) true or false if you show up to class every day and do all of the homework on time and you get an A in this class?
      Solution.
      It is true. When both the hypothesis and the conclusion are true, we have kept the promise.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Is \(S\) true or false if you show up to class every day and do all of the homework on time but you do not get an A in this class?
      Solution.
      It is false. When the hypothesis is true but the conclusion is false, we have broken the promise.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is \(S\) true or false if I do not always show up to class and do not always get my homework in on time, but I still get an A in this class?
      Solution.
      It is true. When the hypothesis is false but the conclusion is false, we have not broken the promise.
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Is \(S\) true or false if I do not always show up to class and do not always get my homework in on time and I get a C in this class?
      Solution.
      It is true. When the hypothesis and the conclusion are false, we have not broken the promise.

      🔗
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection 5.3.B Proof Format: Direct Proof of a Conditional

    Since \(P \to Q\) promises that if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is true, our proof format begins by assuming that \(P\) is true and concludes once we have shown that \(Q\) is true. The proof does not mention the case where \(P\) is false since \(P \to Q\) is automatically true if that happens, as stated in Remark 5.3.5.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.12. Proof Format: Conditional (Direct Proof).

    We can prove \(P \to Q\) by adapting the following proof format.
    🔗
    Proof.
    Assume \(P\) is true.
    🔗
    (Explain why it follows that \(Q\) is true.)
    🔗
    Thus \(Q\) is true.

Activity 6.4.C. Uniqueness proofs.

 
  1. Copy the following direct proof. For a fixed integer \(a\text{,}\) prove that the equation \(2x+1 = a\) has at most one integer solution.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume
    \begin{equation*} 2x+1 = a\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Subtract one from each side of the equation to get
    \begin{equation*} 2x = a-1\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Divide each side of the equation by two to get
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{a-1}{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus, if \(x\) is a solution, then \(x = \frac{a-1}{2}\text{.}\) It follows that \(x = \frac{a-1}{2}\) is the only possible solution.
    🔗
    Therefore, the equation \(2x+1=a\) has at most one integer solution, namely \(x = \frac{a-1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note: There will be one solution if \(a\) is odd and \(\frac{a-1}{2}\) is an integer. There will be no solutions if \(a\) is even and \(\frac{a-1}{2}\) is not an integer.
    🔗

  3. Copy the following indirect proof. For a fixed integer \(a\text{,}\) prove that the equation \(2x+1 = a\) has at most one integer solution.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume that the integers \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are each a solution of \(2x+1=a\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} 2n_1+1=a \text{ and } 2n_2+1=a. \end{equation*}
    Equating these expressions we get
    \begin{equation*} 2n_1+1 = 2n_2+1. \end{equation*}
    Subtract one from each side of the equation to get
    \begin{equation*} 2n_1 = 2n_2. \end{equation*}
    Divide each side of the equation by two to get \(n_1 = n_2\text{.}\) Therefore, the equation \(2x+1=a\) has at most one integer solution.
    🔗
